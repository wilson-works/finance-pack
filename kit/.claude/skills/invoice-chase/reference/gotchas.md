# Gotchas

Known failure modes. Check every draft batch against this list before presenting to the owner — each one has burned a real business relationship somewhere.

1. **Disputed invoices — exclude them, always.** If an invoice is under dispute or query (owner note, pending credit memo, an open email thread about the charge), it never goes in the reminder queue. Chasing a disputed invoice torches goodwill and hands the customer a reason to dig in. List it as "disputed — excluded" in the summary table so the owner sees it was deliberate, not missed.

2. **Partial payments change the ask.** If the customer has paid part of the invoice, chase the **remaining balance**, acknowledge the payment received, and say "remaining balance of [Amount]" — never the original face value. A reminder demanding the full amount from someone who already paid half reads as sloppy at best, hostile at worst.

3. **Net terms: days late counts from the due date, not the invoice date.** A Net-30 invoice dated June 1 is not late on June 2 — it is due July 1 and late on July 2. Always compute lateness from QuickBooks' due-date field; never infer it from the invoice date. Miscounting this turns the skill into one that dunns customers who owe nothing yet.

4. **Payment plans — don't chase.** An invoice on an agreed installment plan shows as overdue in AR aging, but the customer isn't late. Exclude any invoice the owner has flagged as being on a plan. If a customer's payment pattern suggests an arrangement you don't know about (regular partial payments), list the invoice under "needs owner review" instead of drafting a reminder.

5. **Currency and format mismatches.** The amount in the email must match the invoice's currency and formatting exactly — `$1,200.00` is not `€1.200,00`. Never convert to the owner's home currency in the draft. A USD reminder for a EUR invoice creates confusion, a support thread, and a slower payment.

6. **Never threaten legal action in an automated draft.** No "collections," "legal action," or "attorney" at any tier — including final-notice. Escalating to legal is an owner decision made with counsel, not template language. The final-notice tier's job is to say this is the last automated reminder; what happens next belongs to the owner personally.
